# The 12 Laws of Good Bread

A short, friendly handbook for anyone who wants to pull something warm out of
their own oven. No competition, no gatekeeping — just flour, water, salt, time
and a little patience.

---

## Law 1: Weigh, Don't Scoop

A cup of flour is a guess; 125 grams is a fact. Scooping straight from the bag
can pack in fifty extra grams without you noticing, and that is the difference
between a soft crumb and a dense one. Keep a small kitchen scale beside the
mixing bowl and use it every single time.

---

## Law 2: Hydrate Before You Knead

Mix the flour and water alone, then walk away for thirty minutes. That quiet
rest, sometimes called an autolyse, lets the flour soak up water evenly and
starts gluten forming on its own. When you finally do knead, it takes minutes
instead of a quarter hour, and the dough feels relaxed rather than fighty.

---

## Law 3: Salt Is Not Optional

Skipping salt because you are watching your sodium gives you a loaf that tastes
of nothing. Salt tightens the gluten so the dough holds its shape, and it calms
the yeast so the rise does not race ahead of the flavour. Add it after the first
few minutes of mixing and use about two percent of the flour weight.

---

## Law 4: Trust the Long Cold Rise

A dough that spends a night in the fridge bakes better than one you rush out in
the afternoon. Cold slows the yeast but leaves the enzymes working, and that
slow work builds the wheaty, complex taste people chase in bakery loaves. Bake
straight from the fridge if you like — cold dough is actually easier to score.

---

## Law 5: Watch the Dough, Not the Clock

Recipes print times, but your kitchen is not the recipe tester's kitchen. The
same dough that doubles in two hours on a warm August afternoon can take five
on a cold January morning. Look for the signs instead: puffy, rounded on top,
jigelly like a water bed, and leaving a slow dent when you press it gently.

---

## Law 6: Feed Your Starter Regularly

A sourdough starter is two living things in a jar, and they get hungry. Feed it
roughly equal parts flour and water by weight whenever it has doubled and
started to fall, and keep it somewhere steady rather than dramatic. A starter
you neglect for a week is recoverable — it just needs a few patient feeds and
some smell-and-watch judgement before it bakes again.

---

## Law 7: Handle the Dough Gently

Every bubble you crush in the final shape was made by weeks of careful
fermentation, so treat it like something worth keeping. Use a lightly floured
bench, a calm bench scraper, and hands that move with intention rather than
force. A tender loaf with an open crumb almost always comes from a baker who
decided to stop fiddling.

---

## Law 8: Score With Confidence

A slash is an invitation, not a decoration: it tells the loaf where to open so
it does not tear somewhere ugly on its own. Use a fresh blade, commit to the
angle, and drag in one decisive motion instead of sawing. Hesitation gives you
a ragged burst; a confident cut gives you a ear that curls up proudly.

---

## Law 9: Keep a Clean Bench

Flour crusted under your fingernails and dough scraps dried onto the scraper
are how one batch secretly turns into tomorrow's batch. Scrape and wipe as you
go rather than saving the mess for the end. A clean surface also shows you
exactly how sticky your dough really is, which is information you need.

---

## Law 10: Preheat Like You Mean It

Home ovens lose a surprising amount of heat the moment you open the door, so
preheat well past the point where it feels ready. Give the stone or steel at
least forty-five minutes to soak up heat all the way through. A properly
preheated oven is what gives you that dramatic first leap — the oven spring
every beginner photographs.

---

## Law 11: Embrace the Steam

Water vapour in the first minutes keeps the crust soft and glossy long enough
for the loaf to finish expanding. Without steam the surface sets early and the
loaf stops growing, which is how you get a thick, pale, cracked crust. A
preheated tray of boiling water on the bottom rack, or a dutch oven with its
lid on, does the job in almost any kitchen.

---

## Law 12: Never Throw Away a Failed Loaf

A flat loaf still tastes wonderful toasted with butter. Over-proofed dough
becomes excellent focaccia, dinner rolls, or breadcrumbs, and a starter that
went off simply needs feeding. Every baker has a drawer of ugly loaves; the
only real mistake is concluding from one bad bake that you cannot do this.
